How It Works

Offer documents follow the structure set by SEBI’s ICDR Regulations, so the same sections appear in every DRHP. IPOView uses that structure.

1. Map and Read

It extracts the text of every page, finds where each standard section starts, pulls out issue details it can recognise, and lists the numbered risk factors with their pages.

2. Scan for Red Flags

It searches for terms that deserve a closer look, such as pledged shares, qualified audit opinions or regulatory actions, and marks mentions that read like routine “none” disclosures.

3. Compare, Rate and Decide

You enter the key figures and peers. IPOView benchmarks the company against them, then gives a listing-day score and a long-term score out of 10 with a plain-English crux. You add the human judgement.

Automatic extraction depends on how each PDF is laid out, so treat every detected value as a pointer to check, not a final figure. This is a learning tool, not investment advice.

Learn the IPO Basics

A five-minute, plain-English crash course. These examples are general and aren’t linked to the analyser.

First things first

What Happens in an IPO?

An initial public offering (IPO) is when a private company sells shares to the public for the first time and lists on the stock exchange. Some of the money can go to the company (a fresh issue) and some to existing shareholders who are selling (an offer for sale).

Before anyone can apply, the company must publish an offer document with SEBI that discloses its business, financials, risks and legal cases. That document is what IPOView helps you read.

Idea 1

DRHP vs RHP

The DRHP is the draft filed with SEBI for review. The RHP is the updated version issued just before the IPO opens.

Simple example
In a DRHP, the price and number of shares often appear as “[●]”. By the RHP stage, the price band and bid dates are announced.
Why it matters
Use the DRHP to study the business early; use the RHP to check the final numbers and valuation before you apply.
Idea 2

Fresh Issue vs Offer for Sale

A fresh issue creates new shares and the money goes into the company. An offer for sale (OFS) is existing owners selling their shares, so the money goes to them.

Simple example
In a ₹1,000 cr IPO with ₹500 cr fresh issue and ₹500 cr OFS, only half the money helps the business grow.
Why it matters
A mostly-OFS IPO is an exit for existing investors. Ask why they are selling, and at what price they bought.
Idea 3

Reading Subscription and GMP

Subscription shows how many times each investor category bid for its quota. QIBs are institutions, NIIs are high-net-worth investors and retail is everyone applying up to ₹2 lakh.

Simple example
If retail is subscribed 10 times, each applicant has roughly a 1 in 10 chance of getting one lot, because retail allotment is done by lottery when oversubscribed.
Why it matters
Strong QIB demand is a meaningful signal. GMP is an unofficial grey-market quote that SEBI doesn’t regulate, so treat it as a mood, never as a reason.
Price Band and Cut-Off Price

The price band is the range within which you can bid, announced before the IPO opens. Retail investors can tick “cut-off” to bid at whatever final price is set, which improves the chance that the bid is valid.

Lot Size

The minimum number of shares you must apply for, and the multiples you can apply in. Your application amount is the lot size times the price.

Anchor Investors

Large institutions that are allotted shares a day before the IPO opens. Their shares are locked in for a period after listing, so a strong anchor book is read as a vote of confidence.

Basis of Allotment

How shares are shared out when an IPO is oversubscribed. For retail, the aim is to give as many applicants as possible one minimum lot, chosen by lottery.

Listing Gains

The difference between the issue price and the price at which the shares open on listing day. They can be negative too.
